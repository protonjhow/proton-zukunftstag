# Exec brief: drop in on the lemonade company

**When:** Thursday 12 November, Session 3 (14:00–15:30). There's no fixed slot, so drop in whenever suits you.
**Who:** a small group of kids aged 10–12 (currently four girls) on Zukunftstag, the national day when kids spend a day at a workplace. They'll write a school report about the day.

## What they're doing

They're running a **real lemonade business** for the office:

1. They borrowed **CHF 10 from "the bank"** (the finance team) and signed a loan slip: CHF 11 back by the end.
2. They shopped at the Migros downstairs, tracked every franc, and checked what ready-made drinks cost.
3. They asked staff what they'd pay and set a price.
4. They made the lemonade and are selling it to the office (cash or TWINT).
5. At the end they pay back the bank and split the profit.
6. Then the twist: "**Tomorrow every cup is free.** How do you pay the bank?" They work through adverts, selling what they know about customers, charging for extras, and **borrowing more to give it away until everyone's hooked**.

Earlier today they cracked secret messages with a "VPN", and a quiz showed what it noticed about them without asking.

## Best moments to drop in

| When | Roughly | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **The sale** | 14:45–15:08 | **Buy a cup!** Then ask: "How did you choose your price?" "Who lent you the money?" "Will you make a profit?" |
| **Give it away?** | 15:13–15:25 | Join the talk about free lemonade, and answer "how does Proton pay for things?" (below) |

If you can only do one, **buy a cup**. Being a real customer of the boss is the moment they'll write about.

## If they ask "how does Proton pay for things?"

Use their stand:

1. "Your lemonade cost real money: lemons, sugar, and the bank wanted its money back. Proton costs money too: people like the ones you met today, computers, electricity."
2. "Lots of free apps pay with adverts and with what they learn about you, just like the ideas on your 'give it away' sheet."
3. "Some companies borrow lots of money and give things away until everyone's hooked, then start charging. That's the 'borrow more' card."
4. "We chose your first idea: **a fair price**. Some people pay a little every month, and that pays for everyone, even people using Proton for free. So we never need to sell anyone's information, and we can't read their messages anyway, because they're locked."

The core point: **some people pay, so nobody has to be the product.**

## Tips for 10–12 year olds

- Use their own numbers: their price, their profit, their loan.
- Ask them back: "Would you rather pay CHF 1 or see an advert on your cup?"
- Say "lock" and "key", not "end-to-end encryption".
- Praise every question.

## Likely questions

| They ask | A short answer |
|---|---|
| How much money does Proton make? | Your call. "Enough to pay everyone and keep building" is fine. |
| Did Proton borrow money from a bank too? | Your call, in one sentence, kid-sized. |
| Is Proton free? | There's a free version. People who pay keep it running for everyone. |
| Can you read my emails? | No. They're locked with a key only you have. |
| Why don't you just show adverts? | Adverts work best when you know lots about people. We don't want to know. |
| Is TikTok / Roblox / YouTube bad? | Not bad. It's smart to know how they pay for themselves. |
| What does a boss do all day? | Your own day, in a sentence or two. |
| How did you get your job? | Your story. They love this one. |

## Please don't

- Tell scary stories.
- Make kids feel bad about apps they or their families use.
- Pitch the product. Let the lemonade do the work.
